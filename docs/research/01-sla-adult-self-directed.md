# What research says about adult self-directed language learning

Ticket 01. Written for a senior engineer who is not a linguist. Every claim traces to the paper, book, or institute document that owns it.

**Product frame.** Davio is a hobby **B1–B2 German Gym** for one adult Learner living in Germany. Immersion is already free. v1 is curated reading/listening with **Noticing** (“explain this sentence”) and a Grammar-only **Gap map** — not drills, not A1–A2 trees, not more exposure, not capture-from-life. Two-week success = interesting enough to open daily.

## How to read this

Second language acquisition (SLA) is a young field. Ellis presents his pedagogical principles as “provisional specifications,” not prescriptions (Ellis, 2005, pp. 209–211). Lightbown’s anniversary review is the same kind of object: classroom research can set expectations; it cannot dictate a method (Lightbown, 2000). This note follows that standard. Where researchers disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

Davio terms from `CONTEXT.md` are used on purpose. “Input” in the SLA literature is broader than Davio’s **Immersion**; this note keeps both labels and says which is which.

## The one-paragraph answer

Adults do not become usable by flooding themselves with more of the same, or by drilling isolated rules until they feel automatic. They get better when they understand a lot of slightly-hard language **and** periodically notice specific forms in that language, then retrieve those forms later. Krashen’s comprehensible input is necessary and not sufficient (Krashen, 1982, ch. II; Swain, 1985; Long, 1998). Explicit worksheet grammar does not become fluent use by itself (Krashen, 1982, pp. 15–20; Ellis, 2006, pp. 101–102). The thing in the middle — **form-focused instruction in context** — is the research object closest to Davio’s Noticing + Grammar job (Schmidt, 1990; Long, 1991/1998; Ellis, 2006). Spaced retrieval is a real memory effect, mainly proven for discrete items, not for “the German case system” as a whole (Cepeda et al., 2006; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Nakata, 2015). Personalization in the research sense is matching attention to *this* Learner’s current gaps and interests, not an AI path through a tree (Holec, 1981; Ellis, 2005, Principle 9; Krashen, 1982, part 3 of the Input Hypothesis). The B1 stretch is a documented change of job, not a motivation failure: accuracy can dip while the Learner starts using language more independently (Council of Europe, 2020, Grammatical accuracy scale; Richards, 2008).

## 1. Comprehensible input vs explicit instruction

### What Krashen actually claimed

Krashen’s Input Hypothesis is the owning source for “comprehensible input” and `i + 1`:

> a necessary (but not sufficient) condition to move from stage *i* to stage *i* + 1 is that the acquirer understand input that contains *i* + 1, where “understand” means that the acquirer is focussed on the meaning and not the form of the message. (Krashen, 1982, ch. II)

Three further claims in the same chapter matter for product design:

1. The hypothesis is about **acquisition** (subconscious pickup), not **learning** (conscious rules).
2. Understanding of `i + 1` happens with help from context, not from having already acquired the structure.
3. If the acquirer understands enough input, `i + 1` is provided automatically. Deliberately aiming a “structure of the day” at exact `i + 1` is therefore unnecessary and can miss (Krashen, 1982, ch. II, part 3).

The Monitor Hypothesis is the owning source for why rule knowledge often fails in real time. Conscious rules can edit output only when three conditions hold: time, focus on form, and knowledge of the rule. Even then they are not sufficient. Fluent production is initiated by the acquired system; learning is an editor (Krashen, 1982, ch. II, Monitor Hypothesis). Heavy Monitor use produces hesitant speech. “Anything less than a real grammar test will not bring out the conscious grammar in any force” (Krashen, 1982, ch. II).

Later Krashen added **compelling** input: not just interesting, but so engaging that “you forget that it is in another language,” a flow state in which a conscious desire to improve is unnecessary (Krashen, 2011). That claim is a conjecture with case-study style evidence. It is useful as a design pressure on *interest*. It is not a licence to drop form work.

### What the field did with that

Almost nobody in SLA now treats input as optional. Ellis’s Principle 6: “Successful instructed language learning requires extensive L2 input.” If the only input is a few weekly lessons from a coursebook, high proficiency is unlikely (Ellis, 2005, pp. 217–218). Nation’s meaning-focused input strand is the same object: learners listen and read mainly for understanding and enjoyment, with 95–98% of running words already known (Nation, 2007, pp. 2–3, citing Hu & Nation, 2000).

The field **does not** accept Krashen’s strong claim that grammar instruction plays no role in acquisition.

- Instructed learners generally reach higher grammatical competence than purely naturalistic learners, even though the *order* of acquisition stays largely the same (Ellis, 2006, pp. 84–85, reviewing Pica, 1983; Long, 1983; White et al., 1991).
- Norris and Ortega’s meta-analysis of 49 unique-sample studies (1980–1998) found focused L2 instruction produces large target-oriented gains; **explicit** types of instruction outperform implicit types; Focus on Form and Focus on Forms both show large effects; effects are durable. Caveat they own: many outcome measures were constrained constructed responses (fill-in, transformation), which favour instruction, and the domain had weak operationalisation (Norris & Ortega, 2000, pp. 417–418, abstract).
- Canadian French immersion is the classic natural experiment against “input is enough.” Learners become fluent and confident in understanding and producing French while focusing on subject matter (Lambert & Tucker, 1972; Swain, 1991, as reviewed in Lightbown, 2000/2003). Grammatical accuracy still lags. Harley (1989) found Anglophone immersion learners failed to acquire the French preterite/imparfait distinction after hours of exposure, then improved after intensive instruction (cited in Ellis, 2005, p. 213). Gender marking is frequent in every French noun phrase and still remains hard (Harley, 1998, as reviewed in Lightbown, 2000/2003). German case and gender are the same kind of redundant, low-salience morphology.

Swain’s Output Hypothesis is the owning counterweight to input-only. From Canadian immersion evidence she argued that producing language, not only understanding it, is needed for grammatical development. Later she named three functions of output: **noticing** (the gap between what you can say and what you need), **hypothesis testing**, and **metalinguistic reflection** (Swain, 1985; Swain, 1995, pp. 125–144). Ellis’s Principle 7 restates this: production forces syntactic processing, lets learners test hypotheses, and helps automatize existing knowledge (Ellis, 2005, pp. 218–219, drawing on Skehan, 1998, and Swain, 1995).

**Translation for Davio.** Immersion already supplies meaning-focused input. The Gym is not competing with Tagesschau for hours. The research job that remains after Immersion is: make specific forms in otherwise-understood language noticeable, and give the Learner a way to retrieve the explanation later. That is not “more exposure.”

## 2. Form-focused instruction in context (closest to Noticing + Grammar)

This is the section that maps onto Davio’s v1 job.

### Schmidt: noticing is the gate from input to intake

Schmidt’s noticing hypothesis is the owning source for Davio’s **Noticing** term, and it is stricter than “pay attention to the text.”

> I conclude that subliminal language learning is impossible, and that noticing is the necessary and sufficient condition for converting input to intake. (Schmidt, 1990, p. 129)

> intake is that part of the input that the learner notices. (Schmidt, 1990, p. 139)

Two distinctions he insists on, which apps routinely flatten:

- **Noticing ≠ understanding a message.** Comprehending meaning does not automatically yield intake for form (Schmidt, 1990, pp. 139–140, contra Krashen’s collapsing of intake into comprehensible input).
- **Noticing ≠ knowing a rule.** Noticing is conscious registration of specific items in the input (this article, this ending, this word order), not awareness of an abstract grammar rule (Schmidt, 2001; Ellis, 2005, p. 212).

Adult-specific claim: incidental pickup of forms that are **not** crucial to the task “appears unlikely for adults.” Paying attention to form “may be necessary for adult acquisition of redundant grammatical features” (Schmidt, 1990, p. 149). German articles, case, adjective endings, and verb-final in subordinates are exactly those redundant features: you can get the gist of a B1 text without encoding *dem*.

Schmidt later softened “necessary and sufficient.” By 2001: some unattended learning may exist but looks limited; “for all practical purposes, attention is necessary for all aspects of second language learning”; “more attention results in more learning” (Schmidt, 2001, pp. 3, 30). The practical product implication does not change.

**App-founder distortion:** highlighting words in a feed, or generating an AI “explanation of this paragraph,” is not Schmidt-noticing unless the Learner consciously registers a **specific form–meaning pairing** (why *dem*, not *den*). Global “I understood the article” is Krashen-comprehension, which Schmidt says is not enough for intake of form.

### Long: Focus on Form vs Focus on Forms

Long coined the distinction that stops people confusing “grammar in context” with “grammar worksheets.”

- **Focus on forms** (plural): synthetic syllabus. The course designer chops the language into items, presents them one at a time in an external sequence, and the Learner’s job is to reassemble them for communication. Grammar Translation, audiolingual drills, TPR-as-structure-of-the-day. “Focus on forms lessons tend to be rather dry… little if any communicative L2 use” (Long, 1998, pp. 35–37; the 1991 chapter is the original naming).
- **Focus on meaning:** immersion / Natural Approach. Grammar as object is largely off the table. Long’s claim, owned by the 1998 paper: adults can learn much grammar incidentally while focusing on meaning, **but** a focus on meaning alone “(a) is insufficient to achieve full native-like competence, and (b) can be improved upon, in terms of both rate and ultimate attainment, by periodic attention to language as object” (Long, 1998, p. 35).
- **Focus on form** (singular): during an otherwise meaning-focused activity, attention is **briefly** shifted to a linguistic code feature **in context**, typically when a problem arises, in an order determined by the Learner’s internal syllabus — not the textbook writer’s (Long, 1991, pp. 39–52; Long, 1998, p. 35).

Ellis’s three-way table is the clean operationalisation (Ellis, 2006, p. 100, adapted from Ellis, 2001, p. 17):

| Type | Primary focus | Distribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus on forms | Form | Intensive (one structure, many times) |
| Planned focus on form | Meaning | Intensive (pre-selected form inside a communicative task) |
| Incidental focus on form | Meaning | Extensive (many forms, as they come up) |

Davio’s “explain this sentence” is **planned or incidental focus on form**, depending on whether the Gym pre-selects the puzzle. It is not Focus on Forms unless the Activity becomes “today we practise dative after *geben*, ten isolated sentences.”

Ellis’s own conclusion, labelled as his beliefs: a traditional approach of “explicit explanations and drill-like practice is unlikely to result in the acquisition of the implicit knowledge needed for fluent and accurate communication” (Ellis, 2006, pp. 101–102). Grammar should emphasise form **and** meaning/use; teachers should target structures known to be problematic rather than the whole grammar; grammar is best taught to learners who already have some ability to use the language (i.e. not complete beginners); explicit knowledge is worth teaching as a primer for later implicit knowledge; incidental focus-on-form is of special value because it treats many problems extensively (Ellis, 2006, pp. 102–103, beliefs 1–3, 7–8).

That last cluster is almost a spec for a B1–B2 Gym: the Learner is past beginner; do not teach the whole grammar; explain in context; treat many forms as they appear.

### Weak interface: why an explanation can still help fluency later

Three theoretical positions on whether knowing a rule helps you acquire it (Ellis, 2005, pp. 214–216; Ellis, 2006, pp. 95–97):

- **Non-interface** (Krashen, 1981/1982): learned and acquired knowledge are separate. Rules never become the acquired system. Product implication: zero grammar.
- **Strong interface** (DeKeyser, 1998): explicit knowledge becomes implicit through plentiful communicative practice (skill-building / PPP). Product implication: present, then drill in meaning-bearing tasks until automatic.
- **Weak interface** (Ellis, 1993): explicit knowledge does not morph into implicit knowledge directly. It **primes noticing and noticing-the-gap**. Once you can name the pattern, you are more likely to detect it in later input and to compare it with your own output (Ellis, 2006, p. 97, citing Schmidt, 1990). Product implication: a short, in-context explanation plus later encounters in real texts — not worksheets-until-automatic.

Davio’s stated Grammar job (“explanations-on-the-spot, patterns in real texts/audio, Gap map — not drills until automatic”) is the weak-interface bet. The research does not prove that bet; it is one of three live positions. The evidence that *some* explicit attention beats input-only is stronger than the evidence for any one interface story (Norris & Ortega, 2000; Ellis, 2006, pp. 84–85).

### Consciousness-raising vs drills

Ellis’s definition of grammar teaching is broader than present-then-practise:

> Grammar teaching involves any instructional technique that draws learners’ attention to some specific grammatical form in such a way that it helps them either to understand it metalinguistically and/or process it in comprehension and/or production so that they can internalize it. (Ellis, 2006, p. 84)

He explicitly includes: presentation without practice; practice without presentation; **learners discovering rules for themselves**; input flooded with exemplars; corrective feedback during a communicative task (Ellis, 2006, pp. 83–84). Consciousness-raising tasks (Ellis, 1991; Fotos & Ellis, 1991) are the discovery variant: data in front of you, induce the pattern. That is closer to “why was it *dem* in this sentence?” than to a four-option cloze.

Lightbown’s generalisation 4, still standing in the 2000 update: “Practice does not make perfect” — meaning **isolated pattern drill**, not practice broadly defined. Memorised accuracy can collapse into U-shaped behaviour when the Learner starts generating novel sentences (Lightbown, 2000/2003, generalisation 4). Generalisation 6: isolated explicit error correction usually does not change spontaneous use; feedback helps when it is focused on something the Learner can actually learn **and sustained over time** (Lightbown, 2000/2003, generalisation 6).

**Translation for Davio.** A Gap map that records “this form is still a gap” and then re-encounters it inside later Activities is closer to Lightbown’s “sustained over time” than a one-shot explanation. A drill pack that scores 10/10 on dative articles is the thing Lightbown and Ellis both say will not show up in spontaneous use.

## 3. Repetition vs variety

These are not opposites in the research. They are two conditions that have to be true at once.

### Repetition: you do not learn a feature from one meeting

Nation’s vocabulary work is the owning quantitative source, and it generalises to other language features.

- Incidental gains from a single meaning-focused reading are small; they depend on **large quantities of input** to get enough repetition (Nation, 2007, p. 3, citing Waring & Takaki, 2003).
- Nation and Wang (1999), cited in Nation (2007, p. 3): learners needed to read at least one graded reader a week to meet words often enough.
- A common working range in this literature is on the order of **5–16+ meetings** for a word to have a serious chance of being learned, with “at least 12 would be better” as Nation’s later rule of thumb for incidental learning (Nation, 2001, ch. 3; Nation’s later book-level analyses). Zipf’s law makes this painful: in any one authentic text, a large share of word types occur once (Nation, various; see also Nation, 2007, meaning-focused input conditions).
- Quality of meeting matters as well as count: retrieving a meaning you have met before beats merely noticing it; meeting the item in a **new context** beats meeting it in the same sentence again (Nation, later pedagogical summaries of the same research programme).

Ellis’s Principle 1: proficiency needs **both** a rich store of formulaic chunks (for fluency) **and** rule-based knowledge (for complexity and accuracy). Native speakers use far more formulaic language than even advanced L2 learners (Foster, 2001, cited in Ellis, 2005, pp. 210–211). Early instruction that only teaches abstract rules is as likely to produce memorised patterns as analysed rules — and those patterns are still useful (Ellis, 2005, pp. 210–211).

### Variety: the same item in new contexts, not a new tree every day

Krashen’s part 3 of the Input Hypothesis is an argument **against** a tightly sequenced “structure of the day.” Roughly-tuned input (a net around current competence) provides built-in review: `i + 1` recurs without anyone programming it (Krashen, 1982, ch. II). That is variety of *messages* with repetition of *structures*.

Nation’s four-strand course is the owning balance rule: roughly equal time to meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning, and fluency development. Language-focused learning “should not make up more than one-quarter of the time spent on the whole course” and must not become the whole course (Nation, 2007, pp. 2, 6–7). Three of the four strands are meaning-focused, so about three-quarters of time is messages, one-quarter is deliberate study (Nation, 2007, p. 8, aligning with Ellis, 2005, Principles 2 and 3).

Interleaving (mixing items) versus blocking (one structure for a whole sitting) is a general memory finding (related desirable-difficulty work; Rohrer and others). SLA does not have a clean “always interleave grammar” meta-analysis comparable to Cepeda on spacing. The closest SLA objects are Ellis’s intensive vs extensive instruction (Ellis, 2006, question 5; Ellis, 2005, p. 213): some structures seem to need a burst of intensive attention (Harley, 1989: eight weeks on two French tenses); extensive incidental focus-on-form treats many structures and is more individualised. Ellis’s belief 8 prefers incidental/extensive as *specially* valuable, while still allowing intensive work (Ellis, 2006, p. 102).

**Coverage, not “native news.”** Hu and Nation (2000): for unassisted pleasure reading, learners needed very high lexical coverage; 98% known words was the figure that became pedagogical orthodoxy (Hu & Nation, 2000, *Reading in a Foreign Language* 13(1), pp. 403–430). Nation (2007) uses 95–98% as the condition for the meaning-focused input strand. A 2023 replication did not fully recover the 98% threshold in a different population (Language Learning 73(4)) — treat 98% as a design heuristic, not a law. The product implication is stable: B1–B2 **curated** text is the coverage condition; raw Tagesschau is often below it, which is why v1 is a Gym rather than an Immersion importer.

**Translation for Davio.** Variety of adult topics is a motivation and a coverage play (new contexts for the same forms). Repetition of *forms* across those topics is the learning play. A new interesting text that never re-meets yesterday’s *dem* is Krashen-input without Schmidt-intake. The same dative cloze ten times is repetition without variety, which Lightbown says does not make perfect.

## 4. Spaced retrieval

Three separate effects get sold as one “SRS = language learning” feature.

### Spacing (distributed practice)

Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer (2006) meta-analysed 839 assessments in 317 experiments. Spacing beats massing for verbal recall. The **optimal gap between study episodes grows as the desired retention interval grows** (Cepeda et al., 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), pp. 354–380). Cramming wins on a test 5 minutes later; spacing wins on a test days or weeks later.

### Retrieval (the testing effect)

Roediger and Karpicke (2006): taking a memory test improves later retention more than restudying the same material, even without feedback. Restudy wins at 5 minutes and **inflates confidence**. Prior testing wins at 2 days and 1 week (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, *Psychological Science* 17(3), pp. 249–255). Testing is a learning event, not only an assessment event.

### Expanding vs equal intervals (the Anki myth)

App marketing treats expanding intervals (1 day, 3 days, 7 days…) as the scientifically optimal schedule. Nakata (2015), first L2 vocabulary study to find a significant expanding-over-equal advantage, still reports it as **limited**. The **amount** of spacing produced large effect sizes; expanding vs equal was a small extra (Nakata, 2015, *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 37(4), pp. 677–711). Other L2 studies often find expanding and equal roughly equivalent. Massed (zero spacing) is the condition that clearly loses.

Bahrick’s long-horizon work on foreign-language paired associates (Bahrick, 1979; Bahrick & Phelps, 1987; Bahrick et al., 1993) is the reason people believe in multi-year spacing. Cepeda et al. (2006, pp. 358–360 in the review discussion) flag a confound in those designs (more relearning trials at longer ISIs) but note the qualitative pattern matches cleaner studies: for retention of six months or more, ideal ISIs are on the order of a month or more.

### What this does **not** say

Almost all of this evidence is **paired associates, word lists, facts, short prose recall** — discrete items. It is not evidence that an algorithm can space “the German subordinate-clause verb-final rule” as if it were a flashcard. Grammar as implicit knowledge is a different object (Ellis, 2005, Principle 4). Spaced retrieval of an **explanation** or of a **noticed sentence** is a reasonable extrapolation. Spaced retrieval of isolated article paradigms as the core loop is Focus on Forms plus SRS, which is what Ellis says is unlikely to yield implicit knowledge for fluent use (Ellis, 2006, pp. 101–102).

Barcroft (2007) and related L2 work show retrieval practice helps **lexical** learning. That supports a vocab SRS. Davio’s v1 Gap map is Grammar-only. Do not smuggle a vocab-SRS finding into a grammar-Gym spec without calling it an extrapolation.

**Translation for Davio.** If the Gym asks the Learner, days later, to explain *this* sentence’s *dem* again — or to notice the same pattern in a new text — that is retrieval + spacing applied to Noticing. If the Gym is a Leitner box of de/den/dem drills, that is the founder myth.

## 5. What personalization actually means

### Research sense (Council of Europe, 1979/1981)

Holec, writing for the Council of Europe Modern Languages Project, defined learner autonomy as **“the ability to take charge of one’s own learning”** (Holec, 1981, p. 3). Taking charge means the Learner determines objectives, contents and progression, methods, monitoring, and evaluation (Holec, 1979/1981, ERIC ED192557 abstract; Holec, 1981). Autonomy is a **capacity**, not a behaviour; the behaviour is self-directed learning.

That is personalization as **agency**: this adult decides what counts as a gap and what to do next. It matches a Gap map the Learner can mark, not a placement test that assigns a tree.

Ellis’s Principle 9: instruction needs to take account of individual differences — aptitude match and motivation. Dörnyei’s line, quoted by Ellis: “the best motivational intervention is simply to improve the quality of our teaching” (Dörnyei, 2001, p. 26, in Ellis, 2005, p. 220). For a one-Learner hobby product, “quality” collapses into: interesting Activities and instructional clarity.

Krashen’s part 3 is an argument **against** fine-grained adaptive targeting of `i + 1`. Roughly-tuned input covers `i + 1` without knowing the exact next structure; deliberate aim might miss; natural input provides built-in review (Krashen, 1982, ch. II). Ellis (2005, pp. 215–216) calls exact developmental targeting “probably impractical”: teachers have no easy way to know each Learner’s stage, and instruction a bit ahead can still push learners along the built-in syllabus (Pienemann’s teachability hypothesis is the stricter version: teach the next stage, not a random jump — Pienemann, 1988, 1999, as reviewed in Lightbown, 2000/2003, generalisation 3).

Incidental focus-on-form is individualised in a different sense: it responds to the errors **this** person actually produces (Ellis, 2005, p. 213; Ellis et al., 2001). A Gap map filled by probes and by the Learner marking explanations is that object, encoded.

### Marketing sense (flag)

| Marketing phrase | What the research object actually is |
| --- | --- |
| “AI personalizes your path” | Usually a skill tree (Focus on Forms) plus item-response or heuristic unlocking. Not Holec autonomy. Not Long Focus on Form. |
| “Adapts to your level” | Krashen says you cannot reliably aim at exact `i + 1`; Nation says you *can* aim at 95–98% known vocabulary for reading. Coverage-tuning of texts is real; “next grammar node” is mostly a syllabus. |
| “Spaced repetition algorithm unique to you” | Scheduling discrete flashcards. Valid for vocab items (Cepeda; Nakata). Not a model of grammatical competence. |
| “We’ll diagnose your CEFR level” | Placement. CEFR descriptors are **can-do** statements, not a curriculum (Council of Europe, 2020). A B1 badge does not tell you whether *dem* after *helfen* is a gap. |
| “Streaks keep you motivated” | Extrinsic wrapper. Ellis/Dörnyei: quality of the teaching/content is the motivational intervention that research actually talks about. Roediger & Karpicke: restudy inflates confidence without improving delayed retention — the same failure mode as a streak that rewards opening, not retrieving. |

**Translation for Davio.** CONTEXT.md already marks Personalization as a hypothesis whose first concrete shape is the Gap map. That is the research-aligned shape: a picture of forms this Learner does not control, filled by in-Activity probes and self-marks, used to choose what to notice next. It is not an A1–A2 tree with a B1 skin.

## 6. The post-beginner / B1 plateau

### CEFR owns the “accuracy dips at B1” finding

B1 is the Council of Europe **Threshold / independent user**. The Companion Volume’s grammatical-accuracy commentary is the owning source for the plateau-as-accuracy-dip:

> research in English, French and German suggests that inaccuracy increases at around B1 as the learner is beginning to use language more independently and creatively. The fact that accuracy does not increase in a linear manner is reflected in the descriptors. (Council of Europe, 2020, Grammatical accuracy scale notes)

The descriptors themselves (Council of Europe, 2020):

- **B1:** Communicates with reasonable accuracy in familiar contexts; generally good control, though with noticeable mother-tongue influence. Errors occur, but it is clear what they are trying to express. Uses reasonably accurately a repertoire of frequently used “routines” and patterns associated with more predictable situations.
- **B2:** Good grammatical control; occasional slips or non-systematic errors; does not make mistakes which lead to misunderstanding. Has good command of simple structures and some complex forms, though complex structures may be used rigidly with some inaccuracy.

Key concepts on that scale: A1–B1 = control of a **repertoire**; B1–B2 = **prominence of mistakes**; B2–C2 = **degree of control**. The B1→B2 job is not “learn the next 40 app skills.” It is: keep using more independent language **without** the mistake rate dominating, while range expands (Council of Europe, 2020, General linguistic range: B1 still has hesitation, circumlocution, repetition; B2 can express viewpoints and develop arguments with some complex sentence forms).

The same volume notes that when learners tackle more complex tasks, control naturally suffers; “attempting to use more complex language, taking risks and moving beyond one’s comfort zone are essential parts of the learning process” (Council of Europe, 2020, General linguistic range notes). Felt “I’m getting worse” can be the descriptor working as designed.

### ACTFL owns the functional jump, not a tree

ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines 2024 describe what people can **do**, not what they have been taught (ACTFL, 2024). Intermediate: short conversations, simple questions, simple transactions, **sentence-level** communication. Advanced: topics of personal and public interest, narration in major time frames, situations with a complication, **paragraph-level** communication (ACTFL, 2024, overview). Intermediate High can sometimes perform Advanced tasks but cannot sustain them (ACTFL, 2024, Intermediate High speaking). The inverted pyramid is the owning picture of why progress **feels** slower after the beginner burst: each major level is a larger set of functions, contexts, and text types. Mapping to CEFR is approximate (B1 ~ Intermediate Mid/High; B2 ~ Advanced Low/Mid, depending on skill and test). Do not treat either scale as a Davio curriculum.

### Richards owns the “intermediate plateau” as a pedagogical object

Richards (2008, Cambridge booklet) is not a peer-reviewed experiment. It is a synthesis used in teacher education, and it names the phenomenon consumer apps hit:

Typical features of the lower-intermediate → upper-intermediate stretch (Richards, 2008, pp. 1–19):

1. Gap between **receptive** and **productive** competence (you understand language you cannot produce).
2. Fluency may have progressed at the expense of **complexity**.
3. Limited vocabulary range (still around a core 3,000-word level; weak collocations).
4. Persistent **fossilized** errors — lower-level errors that reappear despite time and correction.
5. Inadequate self-monitoring.

On fossilization he cites Lightbown and Spada (2006) and asks the pedagogical question that is Davio-shaped: “How can learners become aware of (notice) the fossilized errors in their own speech?” Suggested direction: more explicit treatment of grammar **within** communicative teaching, plus monitoring (Richards, 2008, pp. 19–21).

Selinker (1972, *IRAL* 10, pp. 209–232) owns the terms **interlanguage** and **fossilization**: some items, rules, and subsystems can persist in the Learner’s system regardless of further instruction. His “5% reach native-like competence” figure is a hypothesis from 1972, not a modern measurement, and is routinely abused in blog posts. Han (2013) updates fossilization as **selective**, not global. Flag the 5% number as an app-founder myth if it appears as “almost nobody gets fluent.” CEFR B2 is independent use, not native-like. Lightbown generalisations 7–8: for most adult learners acquisition stops before native-like mastery; one hour a day will not produce native-like command (Lightbown, 2000/2003). That is a warning against native-news-as-v1-success, not against a B1–B2 Gym.

Ellis’s belief 3 is the timing claim that matches the plateau: “Grammar is best taught to learners who have already acquired some ability to use the language (i.e., intermediate level) rather than to complete beginners” (Ellis, 2006, p. 102). Beginner apps that drop Grammar-in-context just when it would start to pay are doing the opposite of this.

**Translation for Davio.** The plateau is not “the app ran out of tree.” It is: routines that were good enough at A2 are now visible as limited; Immersion is harder than the Study band; accuracy can get worse while range gets better; fossilized case/gender errors will not leave because of more podcasts. The research job at this band is Noticing of specific remaining forms, plus felt evidence of Progress that is not XP.

## 7. App-founder myths (explicit flags)

| Myth | What the owning sources actually say |
| --- | --- |
| Immersion / more input is sufficient once you live in the country | Input is necessary (Krashen, 1982; Ellis, 2005, Principle 6; Nation, 2007). It is not sufficient for grammatical development in adults (Swain, 1985, 1995; Long, 1998; Harley, 1989; Ellis, 2006, pp. 84–85). |
| Comprehensible input means dumbed-down content | Krashen: understand with help from context, roughly tuned, not only `i + 1` (1982, ch. II). Nation/Hu: 95–98% known words for pleasure reading (Hu & Nation, 2000; Nation, 2007). Curated B1–B2 is the coverage condition; baby content is not. |
| Compelling input replaces form work | Krashen (2011) conjectures compelling input as optimal/only true acquisition. Schmidt (1990, p. 149): adults may *need* to pay attention to redundant grammar. Long (1998): meaning-only is insufficient. Davio’s two-week bar *is* an interest/compelling-content bar; the Gym’s job on top of Immersion is still Noticing. |
| Worksheet drills until automatic = fluency | Ellis (2006, pp. 101–102): explicit explanation + drill-like practice is unlikely to yield implicit knowledge for fluent use. Krashen Monitor: rules need time and form-focus and still do not initiate fluent speech (1982, ch. II). Lightbown (2000): isolated drill is the “practice does not make perfect” object. |
| An A1–A2 skill tree continues to work at B1 | CEFR: at B1 inaccuracy can increase as independent use starts (2020). ACTFL: Intermediate → Advanced is a change of text type and function, not the next node (2024). Ellis (2006, belief 3): grammar-in-context pays *after* some ability to use the language. |
| Spaced repetition *is* language learning | Spacing and retrieval are robust for discrete verbal items (Cepeda et al., 2006; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Nakata, 2015). They are not a theory of implicit grammar. Expanding intervals are not magically better than equal ones by much (Nakata, 2015). |
| Restudy / streaks / XP = Progress | Roediger & Karpicke (2006): restudy raises confidence and short-term scores; retrieval wins later. CEFR: Progress at B1 is range + control, and control can dip (2020). |
| Personalization = adaptive AI path | Holec (1981): take charge of objectives, content, methods, monitoring. Ellis (2005, Principle 9): aptitude and motivation; quality of teaching. Krashen (1982): do not aim at exact `i + 1`. Item-level SRS scheduling is a narrow, real personalization. A CEFR placement test is not a Gap map. |
| Noticing = any explanation or any highlight | Schmidt (1990, 2001): conscious registration of specific form features in input. Understanding the message is not intake for form. |
| Adults can’t get good at languages / only 5% succeed | Selinker’s 5% is a 1972 fossilization hypothesis, not a CEFR pass rate. Lightbown: native-like mastery is rare and one hour a day will not produce it — different claim from “B2 is impossible.” CEFR B2 is independent use. |
| Native-like should be the v1 target | Out of scope for Davio and unsupported as an MVP bar. ACTFL Distinguished / CEFR C2 are not the B1–B2 Gym’s job. |
| Output is unnecessary if you only want reading/listening | Swain’s noticing function of output still applies: trying to explain a sentence is a form of metalinguistic output (Swain, 1995). Davio v1 can stay reception-primary and still use explanation-as-output. Full speaking/writing as the product job is a different spec. |
| Teach the whole grammar, in order | Ellis (2006, belief 2): focus on structures known to be problematic. Learner built-in syllabus cannot be replaced by a textbook sequence (Corder, 1967; Ellis, 2005, Principle 5; Pienemann, as reviewed in Lightbown, 2000/2003). |

## Implications for Davio MVP

- **The Gym’s research job is Focus on Form, not more Immersion and not Focus on Forms.** Curated B1–B2 reading/listening supplies the meaning-focused base (Nation’s input strand; Krashen’s `i + 1` with coverage). The differentiator vs free Immersion is briefly shifting attention onto specific forms in those texts — Schmidt-noticing, Long Focus on Form, Ellis incidental/planned FFI. Shipping another input feed fails the job even if it is interesting.

- **“Explain this sentence” is a legitimate Activity type, not a gimmick**, if the puzzle is a form–meaning pairing in a real sentence (why *dem*), not a paragraph paraphrase and not a decontextualised rule. That is consciousness-raising / metalinguistic output in context (Ellis, 2006, p. 84; Swain, 1995). It also serves the two-week interest bar (Ellis, 2005, Principle 2: creating pragmatic meaning is intrinsically motivating; Krashen, 2011: compelling beats merely “educational”).

- **Do not make language-focused work the whole product.** Nation’s cap is about one-quarter of course time; three-quarters stays messages (Nation, 2007). A sitting that is only puzzles with no reading/listening is a grammar app. A sitting that is only reading with no Noticing is Immersion with a UI.

- **The Gap map should store forms this Learner does not control, filled by probes and self-marks, then re-meet those forms in new texts.** That is Lightbown’s “sustained over time,” Ellis’s extensive FFI, and Holec monitoring — not a placement test and not drills-until-automatic. Weak-interface theory is the honest bet: the explanation primes later noticing; it does not compile into fluency by itself (Ellis, 1993/2006).

- **Repetition belongs to forms across varied adult texts, not to a skill tree.** Hu & Nation coverage argues for curated Study-band material; Zipf + Nation argue one authentic article will not re-teach yesterday’s pattern. Mix news-ish and life-ish topics for variety of context; keep returning to the same grammatical gaps.

- **Spaced retrieval is worth using on noticed sentences / gap items, not as the core loop.** Cepeda/Roediger/Nakata support asking again later. They do not support replacing the Gym with Anki. Prefer retrieval of an explanation or a new contextual example over massed restudy of the same cloze (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).

- **Felt Progress at B1 should expect non-linear accuracy.** CEFR says inaccuracy can increase at B1 in English, French, **and German** as independent use starts (Council of Europe, 2020). A UI that treats error rate as the only Progress signal will gaslight a Learner who is actually expanding range. Better signals: a gap that used to need the explanation and now does not; the same pattern noticed in a new text without a prompt.

- **Personalization in v1 = Gap map + interesting content, not an adaptive curriculum.** One Learner, no tree. Holec autonomy and Ellis/Dörnyei quality-of-experience are the sources. An AI path through A1–A2 leftovers is the marketing object this ticket was asked to flag.
