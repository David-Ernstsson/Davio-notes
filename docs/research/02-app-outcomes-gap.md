# Why gamified language apps fail to produce fluent speakers

Ticket: `.scratch/discovery/issues/02-app-outcomes-gap.md`  
Date: 2026-08-22  
Scope: first-party Duolingo/Babbel efficacy, independent studies, and reputable educators. Not Reddit-only.

## Question

Why do popular language apps produce streaks without conversation? What do successful self-learners do instead? What minimum practice mix would a new product need to avoid being “another streak app”? Also: when is “varied interesting activities beat repetitive lessons” true, and when is it false, especially at B1–B2?

## Bottom line

Gamified apps can raise scores on placement tests and on reading (sometimes listening) up to about A2 / ACTFL Intermediate Low, if the Learner actually finishes a large amount of content. They do not, on the evidence they themselves publish, reliably produce CEFR B1–B2 spoken interaction. The gap is not “apps teach nothing.” It is that they optimize daily return and recognition of decontextualized sentences, while fluency is a different skill mix: large amounts of meaning-focused input, noticing of form in real messages, pushed output, and fluency practice on already-known language. A product that only varies the *game* (new exercise skins, streaks, XP) stays a streak app. A product that varies *interesting Study-band texts* and then repeats work on those same texts (Noticing, Gap map, easy re-reads) is doing what successful self-learners do.

## What “fluent speaker” means here

The Council of Europe’s CEFR global scale is the bar consumer apps now claim to align to.

- **B1**: understand the main points of clear standard input on familiar matters; deal with most situations while travelling; produce simple connected text on familiar topics; describe experiences and briefly explain opinions and plans.
- **B2**: understand the main ideas of complex text on concrete and abstract topics; “interact with a degree of fluency and spontaneity that makes regular interaction with native speakers quite possible without strain for either party”; produce clear, detailed text and explain a viewpoint.

Source: Council of Europe, *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages*, global scale.

That is a different target from “can tap the right tile” or “can place out of first-semester Spanish on WebCAPE.” Rod Ellis’s tenth principle of instructed SLA is that selected-response and constrained-response tests overstate instructional effects relative to free constructed response — the kind of language use found outside the classroom.

Source: Ellis, “Principles of Instructed Second Language Acquisition,” Center for Applied Linguistics digest (2008), Principle 10, citing Norris and Ortega (2000).

## What first-party research actually claims

### Duolingo

Duolingo’s own efficacy framework asks four questions, in this order: (1) do learners find it fun and return day after day; (2) do they master what the course teaches; (3) can they transfer that to real-life communication; (4) do they reach the intended CEFR level. Question 1 is engagement (time in app, daily return). Question 3 is explicitly about transfer beyond the app.

Source: Duolingo Research blog, “How Does Duolingo Measure Effectiveness?” (17 Oct 2022), <https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-efficacy-research-framework/>.

**Vesselinov & Grego (2012), commissioned by Duolingo.** Volunteer US English speakers studying Spanish for ~8 weeks took the WebCAPE college placement test (vocabulary, reading, grammar — not an oral interview). Completers gained 91.4 points on average, 8.1 points per hour (95% CI 5.6–10.7). The authors estimated a true beginner would need **34 hours on average** (26–49) to reach the WebCAPE cut-off for second-semester Spanish (270 points). Beginners gained more per hour than more advanced users. Motivation mattered: travel-oriented users gained more than “personal interest” users.

Source: Vesselinov & Grego, *Duolingo Effectiveness Study: Final Report* (Dec 2012), <https://comparelanguageapps.com/reports/DuolingoReport_Final.pdf>.

Limits already inside that report: recommended target was 30 hours; many did far less; inclusion threshold was only 2 hours. Completer bias is built in.

**Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Pajak (2020 white paper) / Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Gustafson, Pajak (2021, *Foreign Language Annals*).** US adults who finished beginning-level (through A2) Spanish or French on Duolingo as their only tool, and who reported little prior knowledge, took ACTFL reading and listening tests. Result: **Intermediate Low in reading, Novice High in listening. No other skills were assessed.** Median study time to that checkpoint: **112 hours** overall (Spanish 125, French 99); mean 141. Correlations between hours in app and test scores were “very small and nonsignificant.” Duolingo compared those reading/listening scores to US university students after four semesters and called the courses “equivalent.”

Sources: Jiang et al., Duolingo Research Report DRR-20-04 (14 Aug 2020), <https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/duolingo-efficacy-whitepaper.pdf>; Jiang et al., *Foreign Language Annals* 54(4), 2021, <https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12600>.

**Speaking (Duolingo first-party, 2021).** After Unit 5 (end of A2 content), 258 Spanish/French learners who reported no prior knowledge and no other tools took Pearson Versant. Duolingo’s own headline: **about half** met or exceeded A2 speaking (66% Spanish, 53% French). Subscores include listen-and-repeat, antonyms, short answers, and story retell — not unscripted conversation with a human. Duolingo writes that it is “challenging [itself] to get learners to even more advanced speaking levels.”

Source: Duolingo blog, “How well does Duolingo teach speaking skills?” (1 Jun 2021), <https://blog.duolingo.com/how-well-does-duolingo-teach-speaking-skills/>.

A later Duolingo video-call RCT (Japanese learners of English, 30 days) found that adding AI conversation on top of regular lessons improved Versant speaking more than lessons alone — and both groups remained at **A1** on speaking. That is first-party evidence that the core lesson loop is not enough for spoken proficiency, and that even an extra speaking feature moved beginners only within A1.

Source: Duolingo white paper, “Video Call improves Japanese English learners’ speaking skills” (2025), <https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/Duolingo_whitepaper_language_video_call_improves_speaking_2025.pdf>.

### Babbel

**Vesselinov & Grego (2016), commissioned by Babbel.** 325 self-reported novice Spanish users in Germany and the US studied for two months, then retook WebCAPE. 92% gained points. Average gain: 12.7 points per hour (CI 11–15). Estimated hours to one college semester: **21** overall, **15** for true novices. About 45% moved up at least one college-semester band. 81% *said* Babbel improved their ability to communicate. Advanced users gained less per hour (~10) than true novices (~18).

Sources: Vesselinov & Grego, *The Babbel Efficacy Study* (2016), <https://assets.ctfassets.net/zuzqvf4m2o58/qoYE4HHG3xeejHGbk0cCU/975d7456a85542a38e17aa4d842b47fe/Babbel2016_Efficacy_Study.pdf>; Babbel press release, 29 Sep 2016, <https://www.babbel.com/press/en-us/releases/2016-09-29-Efficacy_Study.html>.

**Loewen, Isbell & Sporn (2020), *Foreign Language Annals*, with a Babbel-affiliated co-author.** 54 English speakers, not in Spanish class, used Babbel ~10 minutes/day for 12 weeks (average ~12 hours). They gained on grammar and vocabulary tests and **0.7 ACTFL oral-proficiency sublevels** on the OPIc. 59% improved at least one oral sublevel. Time on Babbel was the strongest predictor. The authors note Babbel “presents limited opportunities for oral production,” and that transfer from receptive, explicit lessons to Novice/Intermediate oral production is “notable” *at those levels*.

Sources: Loewen, Isbell & Sporn, *Foreign Language Annals* 53(2), 2020, <https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12454>; Babbel/MSU report PDF, <https://assets.ctfassets.net/zuzqvf4m2o58/5ppltZEo3CYcGf7JRBrRup/e09d2538e3fb4a967fbca26170244953/Babbel-Spanish-2018-Study.pdf>; Babbel press release, 10 Jan 2019, <https://www.babbel.com/press/en-us/releases/2019-01-10-Babbel-SPA-2018-Study.html>.

### What first-party evidence does *not* show

- No published first-party study shows typical app-only learners reaching **CEFR B1–B2 spoken interaction** (unprepared conversation, fluency without strain).
- Outcome instruments are often WebCAPE (placement: grammar/vocab/reading), ACTFL reading/listening, or Versant (semi-automated speaking). These are not workplace or social conversation.
- Completers who finish A2 content after ~100 hours are a selected group. First-party papers themselves report huge time variance and weak time–score correlations.
- Gains per hour shrink as the Learner is no longer a novice (both Vesselinov studies). That matches the Plateau the owner already named.

## What independent research shows

**Krashen (2014) on Vesselinov & Grego 2012.** The 34-hour “one semester” figure uses the *mean* 8.1 points/hour; the *median* was 3.9. Only 90 of 156 starters finished. Of 88 whose scores were used, “personal interest or school” users (n = 62) gained 5.7 points/hour versus 17.6 for travel. Completer satisfaction (78.8%) is 66 of 156 starters. Krashen’s pedagogical point: WebCAPE and Duolingo both target *conscious learning*; communicative competence is a different construct.

Source: Krashen, “Does Duolingo ‘Trump’ University-Level Language Learning?”, *International Journal of Foreign Language Teaching* 9(1), 2014, <http://www.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/krashen-does-duolingo-trump.pdf>.

**Loewen et al. (2019), *ReCALL* — independent Duolingo Turkish case study.** Nine experienced language researchers, true beginners, ≥1 hour/week for 12 weeks (~29 hours). They liked flexibility and gamification, but motivation varied and materials frustrated them. After ~29 hours, **only one participant reached 70%** on a first-semester university Turkish achievement test. Time on app correlated moderately with gains. The authors cite prior independent work on persistence and efficacy problems (Lord 2015; Nielson 2011).

Source: Loewen, Crowther, Isbell, Kim, Maloney, Miller & Rawal, *ReCALL* 31(3), 2019, 293–311, <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344019000065>.

**Nielson (2011), *Language Learning & Technology*.** US government employees given Rosetta Stone or TELL ME MORE as stand-alone workplace training. **51% (P1) and 41% (P2) never logged in.** Only one P1 and four P2 participants completed the protocol. Of 82 absolute beginners in P2, only five used the software more than 10 hours. The one 0-beginner who did 200 hours of Rosetta Stone scored Novice-High on an ACTFL OPI and said: “While Rosetta Stone does teach a lot of words, they are not always the words you need to have an actual conversation.” Nielson’s conclusion: treat packages as supplements, not complete solutions; self-study without support produces severe attrition.

Source: Nielson, *Language Learning & Technology* 15(3), 2011, 110–129, <https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/44265>.

**Lord (2015, 2016).** Semester comparison of classroom Spanish vs Rosetta Stone as textbook vs Rosetta Stone alone. Quantitative scores did not differ cleanly (small *n*, unequal time-on-task: classroom ~109 hours vs RS-only ~48). Qualitatively, software-only learners used more English, needed more clarification, and lacked pragmatic/repair strategies. Lord (2016): capable of isolated lexical items at beginning levels; “potentially serious limitations when it comes to fostering communicative competence and oral proficiency.”

Sources: Lord, *Modern Language Journal* 99(2), 2015; Lord, *IALLT Journal* 46(1), 2016, <https://doi.org/10.17161/iallt.v46i1.8552>; University of Florida summary, <https://news.clas.ufl.edu/does-rosetta-stone-work/>.

**Shortt et al. (2023 systematic review of Duolingo research, 2012–early 2020).** 35 included studies. Majority were *design*-focused, quantitative, non-probability samples. Emphasis on “creation of tools rather than the process and outcomes of language learning.” That is a literature-level finding: the field has measured the game more than fluency.

Source: Shortt, Tilak, Kuznetcova, Martens & Akinkuolie, *Computer Assisted Language Learning*, 2023 (online 2021), <https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2021.1933540>.

**Jiang et al. (2021 FLA) on the commissioned-study genre.** Vesselinov & Grego ran similar WebCAPE designs for Rosetta Stone, Duolingo, Babbel, Busuu, and italki, published as company white papers, not peer review. Jiang et al. call the “hours to first semester” framing hard to interpret and “unwarranted” without better controls, comparison groups, and more interpretable proficiency tests — citing Tarone (2015) and van Deusen-Scholl (2015).

Source: Jiang et al., *Foreign Language Annals* 54(4), 2021, literature review section.

## Why the gap exists (mechanisms)

These are complementary, not competing, explanations.

### 1. The product optimizes return, not transfer

Duolingo’s framework puts “fun, engaging, returning day after day” first, and analyzes in-app data (time, lesson types liked). Streaks, XP, gems, and leaderboards are described in Jiang et al. (2020) as devices that “encourage Duolingo learners to return to their lessons.” That is a successful engagement design. It is not a fluency design.

Jiang et al. (2021) found almost no correlation between hours and ACTFL scores among people who *finished* A2 content, partly because learners can complete only the easiest difficulty level of each skill. A later independent analysis of Duolingo (cited in Jiang, Peters et al. / Sudina & Plonsky 2024 discussion) notes that time can be spent on “unproductive activities, like repeating sessions for easy Experience Points.” A streak can grow while competence does not.

Sources: Duolingo efficacy framework (2022); Jiang et al. (2020, 2021); discussion in “The effectiveness of Duolingo in developing receptive and productive language knowledge and proficiency,” <https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/73595>.

### 2. Recognition is not production; constrained response is not conversation

Ellis (2008, Principle 10): instructional effects are largest on multiple-choice and gap-fill, smallest on free production. App lessons are mostly selected or constrained constructed response (tiles, translations, dictation). CEFR B1–B2 spoken interaction is free constructed response under time pressure, with an interlocutor.

Swain’s output hypothesis: producing language makes learners notice holes, test hypotheses, and process syntax in a way comprehension does not. Controlled practice “typically result[s] in output that is limited in terms of length and complexity” and does not give “sustained output” needed for development.

Sources: Ellis (2008), Principles 7 and 10; Nation (2007) summarizing Swain (1985, 1995, 2005); Swain in Nation, “The Four Strands,” *Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching* 1(1), 2007, <https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf>.

Lord’s and Nielson’s learners could label pictures or accumulate words and still fail conversation. That is the same gap as “finished the tree.”

### 3. Time-on-task for the *wrong* skill

Nation’s “time-on-task” principle: you get better at what you spend time doing. If most minutes are translation of isolated sentences, the Learner gets better at that — not at following a German podcast or holding a work chat.

Source: Nation (2007), commonsense justification of the four strands.

Ellis (2008, Principle 6): if the only input is a limited number of weekly lessons based on a course book, high proficiency is unlikely. The same applies to a daily 5-minute tile session. Children need years of massive input for grammatical competence; adults are not exempt from quantity.

### 4. Input is often not meaning-focused, not extensive, and not i+1

Krashen: acquisition happens from comprehensible input slightly above current level (i+1); anxiety and boredom raise an affective filter. Mechanical memorization is a different process.

Source: Krashen, *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition* (1982), free at <https://www.sdkrashen.com>; restated in Kaufmann, “Comprehensible Input,” LingQ blog, <https://blog.thelinguist.com/comprehensible-input/>.

Nation’s meaning-focused input strand exists only if most language is already familiar (95–98% of running words known), the Learner wants to understand the message, unknown items are few, and **quantity is large**. Isolated app sentences fail several of those conditions: they are often not interesting as messages, not 98% known running text, and not large quantity.

Source: Nation (2007), citing Hu & Nation (2000).

Jiang et al. (2021) themselves found **listening a full ACTFL sublevel behind reading** after finishing A2 content — the same pattern as US university programs (Tschirner 2016; Rubio & Hacking 2019). Sentence-level study plus optional Stories is not the same as extensive listening.

### 5. The intermediate Plateau is a different problem from A0→A2

Jack C. Richards: after ~200 hours, many learners feel a breakthrough into “real communication.” After that, progress feels unmarked. The Plateau (lower-intermediate → upper-intermediate) has five documented features:

1. Gap between receptive and productive competence  
2. Fluency may have grown at the expense of complexity  
3. Limited vocabulary range  
4. Production lacks the characteristics of natural speech  
5. Persistent, fossilized errors  

Richards’s recommended lever for (1) is **noticing**: Schmidt’s claim that input becomes intake only when features are consciously registered.

Sources: Richards, *Moving Beyond the Plateau* (Cambridge, 2008), <https://www.lang-works.com/Beyond-the-Plateau-Richards.pdf>; Schmidt, “The role of consciousness in second language learning,” *Applied Linguistics* 11 (1990); Cambridge ELT excerpt, <https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/2015/08/27/bridging-gap-receptive-productive-competence/>.

Vesselinov’s own numbers already show **diminishing returns past novice**. A B1 Learner who keeps doing A1-shaped lessons will feel no Progress — which is the owner’s history with Duolingo, Easy German, and textbooks.

### 6. Transfer-appropriate processing and missing interaction

Ellis (2008, Principle 8): interaction (negotiation of meaning, repair, feedback) is central; Hatch: one learns conversation by doing conversation. App speech recognition that matches a script is not negotiation. Duolingo’s 2020 paper admits interaction is crucial and points to optional meetups — outside the core loop.

Lord: classroom learners practiced repair in the target language; software learners fell back to English.

Nielson: commercial packages often lack culturally authentic, task-based activity (citing Lafford, Lafford & Sykes 2007).

## What successful self-learners actually do

This is not Reddit lore. It is what applied linguists prescribe, and what documented polyglot/self-study methods operationalize.

### The educator baseline: four strands, ~25% each

Paul Nation: a well-balanced course gives roughly equal time to:

| Strand | What it is | Typical activities | Condition that makes it real |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning-focused input | Learn by understanding messages | Extensive reading, stories, TV, being a listener | 95–98% known words; interest; large quantity |
| Meaning-focused output | Learn by conveying messages | Conversation, letters, explaining how to do something | Familiar content; real message; strategies for gaps |
| Language-focused learning | Deliberate attention to form | Intensive reading, word cards, feedback, pronunciation | Deep processing; spaced repetition; items also appear in the other strands; **≤25% of time** |
| Fluency development | Get faster with what is already known | 4/3/2 speaking, repeated reading, easy stories, speed reading | **No unknown language**; meaning focus; pressure to go faster |

Source: Nation (2007); also Nation & Yamamoto, “Applying the Four Strands to Language Learning,” Victoria University of Wellington, <https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/yamamoto-four-strands.pdf> (explicitly applied to *self-regulated* learning without a teacher).

Three of four strands are meaning-focused. Language-focused study that *becomes the whole course* is the failure mode of both grammar-translation textbooks and tile apps.

### Ellis’s instructed-SLA principles (compressed)

Successful instruction: formulaic chunks *and* rules; predominantly meaning, plus focus on form; implicit knowledge as the goal, explicit as a helper; extensive input; opportunities for output (including pushed output); interaction; individual differences; assess free production.

Source: Ellis (2008), Principles 1–10.

Schmidt: noticing specific items in input (form–function mapping), not only abstract rules, converts input to intake. Richards treats this as a Plateau-breaking tool.

### What documented self-learners do with that science

**Steve Kaufmann (LingQ).** First-party method, not a forum post: large daily reading and listening of *interesting* content at i+1; at B1–B2, “hundreds of thousands of words,” learner podcasts / captioned vlogs / graded readers before full-native news; grammar books as *reference to notice patterns*, not the curriculum; speaking delayed until a large passive base exists, then targeted. He states that pure CI does **not** automatically produce advanced speaking — output must be added at intermediate. He also uses heavy *repetition of the same mini-stories* (he has described ~40 listens) at the start — variety of *topics* later, repetition of *texts* early.

Source: Kaufmann, “Comprehensible Input: How It Helps Language Learning,” <https://blog.thelinguist.com/comprehensible-input/>.

**Refold roadmap (cites Krashen 1982, Day & Bamford 1998, Webb 2020, Webb & Chang 2015).** Phase 2 (comprehension): core 2,000 words, then sentence mining from native-ish content the Learner cares about, reading-with-audio, domain focus (narrow input). Phase 3: close the reading–listening gap (Nation 2006), then light pronunciation and crosstalk *before* full speaking. Hour estimates for a “distant” language through Phase 3 run to **hundreds of hours**, not 34.

Sources: <https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-2>, <https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-2/2b-develop-your-comprehension>, <https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-3>, <https://refold.la/roadmap/phase-0/0c-immerse-in-the-language>.

**Olly Richards (StoryLearning).** Stories written to carry the syllabus; read and listen *repeatedly*; then video lessons that *point at* grammar and vocabulary in the story. Variety is in the narrative; repetition is of the same text.

Source: Glossika interview with Richards, <https://ai.glossika.com/blog/glossika-interview-series-olly-richards>.

**Gabriel Wyner (Fluent Forever).** Pronunciation first; Anki for retrieval of words and grammar *already met* in meaningful use (tutor sentences); flashcards as practice of recall, not as a substitute for input. Wyner and Richards both treat SRS as deepening input, not as the course.

Source: Fluent Forever blog, “Why Use Monolingual Flashcards,” <https://blog.fluent-forever.com/why-monolingual-flashcards/>; “Hacking Fluent Forever,” <https://blog.fluent-forever.com/hacking-fluent-forever/>.

**Pattern across methods (and Nation/Ellis):** volume of interesting, mostly-understood input; deliberate noticing of form *inside* that input; retrieval (SRS or re-reading); delayed or separate speaking practice; no belief that a streak of decontextualized items is the path.

## Minimum practice mix to avoid “another streak app”

A new product does not need to *contain* all four strands if the Learner’s life already supplies some of them. It does need to **refuse to pretend** that one strand plus XP is a complete solution.

Nation’s own check: over two weeks, classify every activity and its minutes. If one strand is missing in the Learner’s whole week (app + life), the product or the Learner must add it.

For a B1–B2 German Learner who already lives in Germany:

| Strand | Who should own it | Minimum if a product wants to claim “not a streak app” |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning-focused input | Product can own the *Study-band* slice (curated, 95%+ comprehensible, interesting). Immersion (Tagesschau, work) is already available and often too hard. | Bounded Activities on real-ish texts/audio the Learner wants to understand; quantity over new game types. |
| Language-focused learning | Product can own **Noticing** on those texts (why *dem*, pattern, recall) plus a Gap map. Must stay ≤ ~25% of *study* time, not become drills-until-automatic. | Explain-this-sentence / mark-the-gap; spaced return to the *same* forms in new texts. |
| Fluency development | Easy re-reads/re-listens of yesterday’s Activity; 4/3/2-style retell of a known text. Apps usually skip this because it looks “repetitive.” | At least one “already known, go faster” move per sitting or per week. |
| Meaning-focused output | Conversation, writing, repair. Apps are weak here (Loewen 2020; Lord; Duolingo Versant ~50% A2). Living in Germany supplies opportunities; the Gym need not be the conversation partner in v1. | Honest scope: do not sell conversation from input+noticing alone (Kaufmann; Swain; Ellis Principle 7). If output is in-product, it must be *messages*, not tile speaking. |

**What makes a streak app, mechanically**

- Progress signal = consecutive days / XP / tree completion (Duolingo framework Q1; Jiang 2020 on streaks).
- Core loop = new items in the same constrained item format.
- No requirement that the Learner understood a *message* or can explain a form.
- Hours can be gamed on easy reviews (Jiang 2021 time–score decoupling).

**What the minimum mix must show instead**

- Progress = evidence of competence (a noticed form, a mapped gap, a faster re-listen of a known text) — Ellis Principle 10 / Davio “Progress ≠ XP.”
- Most minutes on meaning-focused Study-band input (Nation; Ellis Principle 6; Krashen/Kaufmann).
- Some minutes on language-focused Noticing *of those same texts* (Schmidt; Richards; Nation ≤25%).
- Some minutes on fluency with *familiar* language (Nation fluency conditions; Bygate/Suzuki task repetition).
- Output either clearly out of scope or actually pushed (Swain), not script-match.

That mix can live in one sitting: Orient → one curated Activity (input) → Noticing probes (language-focused) → optional easy repeat (fluency) → a Progress mark on the Gap map. The missing strand (live output) is then the Learner’s Immersion life, not a fake in-app conversation.

## Challenge: “varied interesting activities beat repetitive lessons”

The slogan conflates three different things. They do not have the same truth value.

### A. Varied *interesting content* vs boring courseware

**True at B1–B2, strongly.** Krashen’s affective filter: boredom blocks acquisition. Kaufmann: at novice, novelty of the language itself is enough; “as you advance, variety and engagement play a more important role”; accepting boring-but-comprehensible content is a stated mistake. Richards: after the first ~200 hours, stimuli are no longer new, motivation drops, Plateau begins. Nation: meaning-focused input *requires* that learners are interested and want to understand. Ellis Principle 9: quality of teaching / intrinsic motivation.

For a B1–B2 Learner who abandoned Duolingo and textbooks, **topic variety and adult interest** are load-bearing, not decoration.

**False if “variety” means jumping to native news.** Kaufmann: authentic media at A1 is noise; at B1–B2 he still recommends learner-focused podcasts, captioned vlogs, graded readers — “not quite authentic-level.” Davio’s own split (Study band vs Immersion) matches this. Variety must stay inside i+1 / 95–98% known words (Nation; Hu & Nation 2000).

### B. Varied *exercise types* vs repeating the same pedagogic move

**Mostly false as a fluency strategy; sometimes true for grammar discrimination.**

- **Fluency wants repetition of the same task/content.** Bygate: repeating an oral task shifts attention from conceptualization to formulation and raises fluency. Lambert et al. / de Jong & Perfetti: same-task repetition improves speech rate. Suzuki (2021, *Language Learning*): **blocked** task repetition (AAA, then BBB) produced better transfer of fluency (articulation rate, shorter mid-clause pauses) than **interleaved** variety (ABC each day). Nation’s fluency strand *forbids* unknown language and requires repeated easy performance with a speed push. Kaufmann’s 40× mini-story is the self-learner version.

  Sources: Suzuki, *Language Learning* 71 (2021), <https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12433>; review in *Languages* 8(4), 2023, <https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8040252>; Nation (2007) fluency conditions.

- **Grammar learning wants interleaving of similar forms, not one-chapter blocking.** Nakata & Suzuki (2019, *Modern Language Journal*): mixing five English structures beat blocked practice on a 1-week delayed grammaticality test, even though interleaving produced more errors *during* training (desirable difficulty). Learners with *lower* pretest scores benefited more — so this is not only an advanced trick. Suzuki, Yokosawa & Aline (2020): interleaved practice aided proceduralization of L2 syntax.

  Sources: Nakata & Suzuki, *MLJ* 103(3), 2019, <https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12581> (PDF: <https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Nakata-Suzuki-2019-MLJ.pdf>); Suzuki et al., *Language Teaching Research*, 2020, <https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168820913985>.

- **App-style variety (new skins of the same tile lesson) is not either of the above.** Shortt et al.: Duolingo research fixates on design/gamification. Changing the mini-game does not add meaning-focused input, noticing, or fluency conditions. That is the streak-app failure mode: it *feels* varied and still does one thing.

### C. Varied activities vs repetitive *lessons* at B1–B2 specifically

| Claim | At A1–A2 | At B1–B2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need highly repetitive, simple, high-frequency input | True (Kaufmann Mini Stories; Nation quantity + 98% coverage) | Less true for *new* texts; still true for fluency on *known* texts |
| Need compelling, interest-aligned content | Helpful but not the bottleneck | True — Plateau / affective filter (Richards; Kaufmann mistake 1) |
| Need to interleave similar grammar (e.g. German case/word order) | After a short blocked introduction (Nakata & Suzuki discuss increasing schedules) | True — this is where fossilized errors live (Richards feature 5) |
| Need new activity *types* every sitting | False (Nielson: beginners asked for *more* guidance and a plan) | False if types are still constrained response; true if types are new *texts* plus Noticing |
| Need task repetition | Builds early fluency | Still required for automatization (DeKeyser via Ellis Principle 4; Segalowitz via Suzuki 2021) |
| Variety of domains (narrow then expand) | Premature | True — Refold domain focus; Krashen narrow reading |

**Resolution for Davio:** “Varied interesting activities beat repetitive lessons” is **true** when it means *varied interesting Study-band texts and topics*, and **false** when it means *novel game mechanics or never repeating a text*. At B1–B2 the winning pattern is **interesting variety of messages + repetitive work on form and fluency inside those messages** (Richards/Schmidt noticing; Nation strands; StoryLearning; Kaufmann re-listening).

## Implications for Davio MVP

1. **Do not compete on streaks.** Duolingo already won engagement (its own Q1). First-party and independent data say that loop tops out around A2 recognition/reading, with listening behind, speaking at best ~50% A2 on a semi-automated test, and weaker gains once the Learner is past novice. Davio’s Progress signal should be Noticing and the Grammar Gap map, not XP.

2. **Own two strands; do not fake four.** v1 as a Gym of curated Study-band reading/listening plus Noticing matches Nation’s meaning-focused input + language-focused learning, and Richards/Schmidt on the Plateau. Immersion already exists and is often too hard. Meaning-focused output (conversation) is what apps fail at and what living in Germany can supply; v1 should not promise fluency from the Gym alone.

3. **Interest is the variety that matters; repetition is the work.** At B1–B2, varied adult topics beat another tree of repetitive tile lessons (Kaufmann, Richards, Nation interest condition). Varied *mini-games* do not. Repeat the same Activity for Noticing and for an easy fluency pass. Interleave German forms that the Learner confuses (cases, word order) across texts — that is Nakata & Suzuki interleaving, not “a new game each day.”

4. **Felt Progress is the product job because the science says the Plateau feels like zero gain.** Richards’s description is the owner’s history. Tiny probes that fill a Gap map are a Progress design that first-party apps did not ship. The MVP bar (open most days for two weeks because it is interesting) is closer to Kaufmann’s compelling input than to a streak threat.

5. **Hours will be large; the app must stay a sitting.** Jiang’s completers needed a median 112 hours to *finish A2 content*. Refold counts hundreds of hours through listening. Davio cannot compress that. It can make 20–40 minutes at a desk (or 5–15 on a phone) feel like a finished Gym sitting on a real text — the opposite of an infinite lesson queue.

6. **Ticket 09 Activity types should be few and deep, not a carnival.** Candidates that survive this note: (a) curated read/listen of a Study-band text; (b) Noticing / “explain this sentence”; (c) optional easy repeat of yesterday’s text. That is enough to not be a streak app. Adding speech tiles or a new game skin would move Davio back toward the genre the evidence says stalls at the Plateau.
