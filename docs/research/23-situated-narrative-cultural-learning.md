# Situated learning, stories, culture, place, and “the system knows what I need”

Written for a senior engineer who is not a linguist. Every claim traces to the paper, book, or institute document that owns it. Recommendations are labelled.

**Product frame.** Davio is a hobby **B1–B2 German Gym** for one adult Learner living in Germany. Immersion is already free. v1 is curated reading/listening with **Noticing** and a Grammar-only **Gap map** — not A1 survival phrases, not a map of Berlin, not a culture-lesson track. Two-week success = interesting enough to open daily. This note is **discovery research**, not a product-experience spec.

SLA, Noticing, Focus on Form, four strands, spacing, and “personalization ≠ skill tree” are already owned in [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md) and [14](14-linguistic-learning-content-application-domains.md). This note **extends** those objects into situated-learning talk, café-as-context, stories, culture, German varieties, maps, exploration mechanics, and adaptive session design. It does not re-litigate them.

## How to read this

Second language acquisition (SLA) still treats pedagogical advice as provisional (Ellis, 2005, pp. 209–211). Where researchers disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

German and learning claims are split:

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **Language** | What is true of German with no Learner in the picture? |
| **Pedagogy** | What is worth this Learner noticing, in this Study band? |

A mixed sentence is two claims. Cultural “how to order coffee in Vienna” factoids with no owner are marked **UNVERIFIED** and stopped. Do not fill the gap with a convenient rule.

`CONTEXT.md` terms (Learner, Gym, Immersion, Noticing, Gap map, Study band, Activity) are used on purpose. “Input” in the SLA literature is broader than Davio’s **Immersion**.

## The one-paragraph answer

A map of Berlin, a café role-play, and a “culture of the week” card are not the research objects they are named after. **Situated learning** is joining a community of practitioners and changing identity through legitimate peripheral participation — not placing vocabulary on a street (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Lave, 1991; Wenger-Trayner & Wenger-Trayner, 2015). **Embedding language in “real-world contexts”** splits into three different things: Council of Europe Threshold *situations* (café, train — a beginner syllabus of settings); **task-based** teaching, whose unit is a real-world *job* identified by needs analysis, not a textbook café (Long, 2015); and **content-based / CLIL** teaching, whose unit is a school subject taught *through* the L2 (Coyle, Hood, & Marsh, 2010). None of those is “decontextualized flashcards lose, therefore a map wins.” At B1–B2 the closer object is already in [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md): interesting, coverage-tuned texts plus brief Focus on Form. **Stories** can make input compelling (Krashen, 2011) and can transport the reader (Green & Brock, 2000); transportation is about belief change and reduced spotting of “false notes,” not proven L2 grammar intake, and StoryLearning® is a commercial brand citing Krashen, not a trial. **Culture** in Byram and the CEFR Companion Volume is intercultural *competence* (skills, attitudes, critique of stereotypes), not a quiz of national traits (Byram, 1997; Council of Europe, 2020). **German** is a language with dialects *and* several **standard** varieties (Germany / Austria / Switzerland); those are Language facts owned by Ammon, the Variantenwörterbuch, Variantengrammatik, and IDS — not Landeskunde colour (Ammon et al., 2004/2016; Elspaß & Dürscheid, 2017; Council of Europe, 2020). No primary study found here shows that a **real city** beats a fictional setting for acquisition or retention. **Exploration / fog of war** is a game-design aesthetic (Hunicke, LeBlanc, & Zubek, 2004; Bartle, 1996; Yee / Quantic Foundry), not a learning-app finding, and the owners do not claim it retains players after novelty fades. **“The system knows what I need today”** is IRT / knowledge tracing / mastery on *items*, which v1 cannot identify ([14](14-linguistic-learning-content-application-domains.md)); matching *interest* is a different object. Session length is not the same as spacing: distributed practice beats massing for discrete verbal items (Cepeda et al., 2006; Serrano, 2022); that does not licence five-minute grammar snacks as a B1–B2 Gym.

## 1. Situated learning / communities of practice

### Pedagogy (what Lave & Wenger actually claimed)

Lave and Wenger’s *Situated Learning* (1991) is an anthropological account of **apprenticeship**, not a method for putting words on a map. The owning process is **legitimate peripheral participation** (LPP): newcomers take part in a community of practitioners and move toward full participation in that community’s sociocultural practices. Mastery of skill is **subsumed** in becoming a participant with an identity in the practice (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 29, as quoted in Lave, 1991, and in Wenger-Trayner & Wenger-Trayner, 2015). The 1991 Cambridge volume was not opened in full here; the claims below are taken from Lave’s own 1991 chapter (which cites the book), from Wenger’s later restatements, and from quoted passages with page numbers.

Three claims that product talk routinely drops:

1. **LPP is not a teaching technique.** “Legitimate peripheral participation is not itself an educational form, much less a pedagogical strategy or a teaching technique. It is an analytical viewpoint on learning” that distinguishes learning from intentional instruction (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 40, as quoted in New Learning Online’s excerpt of the book). You cannot “implement LPP” by shipping a feature.

2. **Situated ≠ physical place.** Lave (1991) is explicit that one common misreading treats “situated” as “cognition plus social factors in a location.” In the interpretive view she discusses, “Situatedness here is not equated with physical locatedness in the world, in places, settings, or environments. It is not possible to walk into a situation.” A Berlin U-Bahn tile in an app is a *setting picture*. It is not a situation in Lave’s sense.

3. **Mastery is relational, not a badge.** In the same chapter, “mastery is an organizational, relational characteristic of communities of practice”; changing skill is subsumed in changing identity through membership (Lave, 1991). A Gap-map chip “I hold *dem*” is Learner-state evidence. It is not Lave-mastery.

Wenger later named **community of practice** along three dimensions: **joint enterprise** (what it is about), **mutual engagement** (the relationships that bind members), and **shared repertoire** (routines, artifacts, vocabulary developed over time) (Wenger, 1998, *Systems Thinker*). A community of practice is “different from a community of interest or a geographical community, neither of which implies a shared practice.” Wenger-Trayner and Wenger-Trayner (2015) restated the three elements as **domain**, **community**, and **practice**, and listed product-shaped myths: a website in itself is not a community of practice; having the same job title is not; “It’s also full of sites that are called a community of practice even if no one is there!”

The 1991 cases are midwives, tailors, quartermasters, butchers, and Alcoholics Anonymous (Cambridge catalog; Lave, 1991). The Learner living in Germany already has Immersion communities (work, neighbours, media). The Gym is a **study sitting**, not a new CoP.

### Common product misreadings

| Slogan | What the owners actually say |
| --- | --- |
| “Learning is situated, so put vocab in a café / on a map.” | Situatedness is participation in a practice, not GPS (Lave, 1991). A map is a content skin. |
| “The app is a community of practice.” | Domain + regular interaction that builds a shared practice (Wenger-Trayner & Wenger-Trayner, 2015). One Learner and a corpus is not that. A forum with nobody in it is explicitly called out as fake. |
| “Newcomers start at the edge of the map (fog of war) = legitimate peripheral participation.” | Peripheral in LPP means *social access to practice* (low-risk but real work, observing old-timers), not unrevealed tiles (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Lave, 1991). |
| “School failed because it wasn’t in the real world; an authentic backdrop fixes that.” | Lave (1991) argues that schools often *fail to be* communities of practice; the critique is institutional, not “add a street scene.” |

**Translation for Davio.** The Learner’s Immersion *is* the situated world. The Gym’s job is Focus on Form in owned texts ([01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md)). Calling a map “situated learning” is a category error. If a later slice ever wanted a CoP, it would be other German-using adults doing a shared practice — not a UI.

## 2. Contextual instruction, CLIL, TBLT: café vs interesting text

Three research programmes get sold as one “context beats lists” slogan. They are different units of analysis.

### Pedagogy — Threshold *situations* (café, train, museum)

The Council of Europe **Threshold Level** (van Ek, 1975; van Ek & Trim, *Threshold 1990*) defined an adult objective in terms of **roles, settings, topics, functions, and notions** — what a traveller/resident should be able to *do* in everyday encounters. Settings and situations are a planning grid for **T-level** (later aligned roughly with independent-user / B1 communication), not a theory of acquisition. Grammar and lexicon are “means to communicative ends,” not the syllabus’s organising unit (van Ek & Trim, 1990, preface).

That is the honest owner of “order in a café / buy a ticket.” It is a **beginner-to-threshold situational syllabus**. It is not Lave, not Long, and not Nation’s meaning-focused input strand.

**Davio cut.** The Learner is past that job and already lives in Germany. Repeating A1 survival phrases in prettier settings is the plateau-app failure mode [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md) already flagged. CEFR B1 reception is “main points of clear **standard** input on familiar matters”; B2 is “main ideas of propositionally and linguistically **complex** discourse” (Council of Europe, 2020). That is texts and talks, not “Kaffee, bitte.”

### Pedagogy — TBLT (Long): a *task* is not a café role-play

Long’s **TBLT** (uppercase) uses **target tasks** identified by a **needs analysis**: real-world communicative activities *this* population must perform. Analysts then sample the language natives actually use on those tasks; **pedagogic tasks** are simpler approximations, sequenced by task complexity, not by a grammar list (Long, 2015, as restated in Long, 2016). Assessment is criterion-referenced *task* performance.

Long’s own warning: commercial “classroom tasks” that role-play a job interview in order to practise questions are **structure-trapping** (Skehan’s term, cited by Long) — Focus on Forms with a communicative skin. Ellis’s “focused” / consciousness-raising tasks are a different object again (Long, 2016). Off-the-shelf café dialogues written from textbook writers’ intuitions are “for all students and for no students in particular” (Long, 2016).

Does “real-world context” beat decontextualized items? Long’s claim is narrower: **input should be relevant to the learner’s target tasks**, elaborated rather than dumbed down, with **Focus on Form** during meaning-focused work — not “any backdrop helps memory.” Most vocabulary at advanced levels cannot be taught item-by-item in class time; it needs repeated exposure in domain discourse (Long, 2016, citing Nation). That is coverage and quantity, not a museum skin.

**Davio cut.** A one-Learner hobby has no occupational NA. The “target task” is already specified: understand Study-band German and notice form. Inventing café modules as if they were Long-tasks is the misreading Long named.

### Pedagogy — CBI / CLIL: a *subject* taught through the L2

**Content-based instruction:** “the concurrent study of language and subject matter, with the form and sequence of language presentation dictated by content material” (Brinton, Snow, & Wesche, 1989, p. vii, as quoted in Cenoz, 2015). Classic owner: Canadian immersion.

**CLIL:** “a dual-focused educational approach in which an additional language is used for the learning and teaching of **both content and language**” (Coyle, Hood, & Marsh, 2010, p. 1). It is “not a new form of language education” and “not a new form of subject education.” It is content-driven school fusion (history *in* German, biology *in* English). Coyle (2007) later split **language of / for / through** learning — still about subject classrooms.

A café map is not CLIL. CLIL/CBI outcomes inherit the immersion finding already in [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md): lots of meaning-focused input can yield fluent comprehension while **low-salience grammar lags** (Harley, 1989; Lightbown, 2000; Swain, 1985). Embedding does not replace Focus on Form for adult redundant morphology (Schmidt, 1990).

### Pedagogy — Nation: *context* means new texts, not a backdrop

Nation’s four strands already owned in [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md): meaning-focused input (read/listen mainly for meaning, ~95–98% known words), meaning-focused output, language-focused learning (≤ ~25% of time), fluency development (Nation, 2007). “Context” here is **meeting items in varied messages**, plus a capped language-focused strand. Decontextualized list study is a legitimate *slice* of language-focused learning for **vocabulary items**; it is not the whole course, and it is not a theory of implicit grammar (Nation, 2007; Nakata, 2015; Ellis, 2006).

Ellis’s belief 3: grammar-in-context pays **after** learners already have some ability to use the language — i.e. not complete beginners (Ellis, 2006, p. 102). That is the B1–B2 timing claim, not an A1 café claim.

### What this does **not** say

There is no clean meta-analysis in the sources opened here that says “café/train/museum settings outperform decontextualized items for B1–B2 form learning.” The evidence that *is* owned:

- Meaning-focused **quantity** of understandable input matters (Krashen, 1982; Ellis, 2005, Principle 6; Nation, 2007; Elley & Mangubhai, 1983).
- Meaning-only is insufficient for adult grammar (Long, 1998; Swain, 1985; [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md)).
- Situational syllabuses are a **Threshold planning tool**, not an acquisition engine (van Ek, 1975).
- “Context” in Nation is **new texts for the same forms**, not a wallpaper.

**Translation for Davio.** Keep adult interesting texts (news-ish / life-ish) as the meaning-focused base. Do not rebuild A1 situations. Form work stays Focus on Form in those texts.

## 3. Narrative / story-based learning

### Pedagogy — what is owned

**Krashen, compelling input (2011).** Input must be comprehensible; interest may not be enough; **compelling** means “so interesting you forget that it is in another language,” a flow state (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990, cited by Krashen). Compelling input “appears to eliminate the need for motivation”; acquisition happens whether you intend to improve or not. Evidence offered: case studies of unexpected improvement after becoming avid readers (Krashen, 2004; Lao & Krashen, 2008). **Conjecture he owns:** compelling stories/movies/conversations may be “not just optimal: It may be only way we truly acquire language.” [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md) already flags this as a design pressure on *interest*, not a licence to drop form work. Schmidt (1990, p. 149): adults may need to attend to redundant grammar.

**Elley & Mangubhai (1983), Fiji “book flood.”** Primary-school ESL. 380 Class 4–5 pupils given 250 high-interest illustrated **story books** vs matched controls on a structured English programme with little reading. After eight months, reading and listening progressed at about **twice** the normal rate; after 20 months gains had spread to related skills (*Reading Research Quarterly*, 19(1), 53–71, https://doi.org/10.2307/747337). Elley (1991) reviews further book-flood work. This is the strongest **story-as-input** trial in the set. Limits they own: children, ESL in a book-poor school system, illustrated graded-ish stories, not adult B1–B2 German form-focused Gym work. It supports “lots of interesting stories beat a dry structural programme for young learners.” It does not show that a single plotted adventure app replaces Noticing.

**Mason & Krashen Story-Listening.** A later **oral** method: tell (often folk) stories with comprehension-aiding supplementation (drawings, gestures, brief translation) rather than pre-teaching lists (Mason, 2022; Krashen, Mason, & Smith, 2018). Owner: beginner-ish EFL listening. Not a B1–B2 reading Gym.

**StoryLearning® (Olly Richards).** A commercial curriculum that cites Krashen: one continuous graded story, then video lessons that unpack grammar from the story (Richards / storylearning.com). The Glossika interview (Richards describing his own method) is explicit that stories are written to **cover a traditional syllabus’s structures**. That is a synthetic syllabus inside a narrative skin — closer to Long’s structure-trapping warning than to Elley’s book flood. Treat as **marketing that cites research**, not as a trial.

**Green & Brock (2000), transportation.** *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79*(5), 701–721, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701. Transportation = absorption into a story (imagery, affect, attentional focus). Higher transportation → more story-consistent **beliefs** and more favourable protagonist evaluations; labelling the story fact vs fiction generally did not matter. Experiment 2: highly transported readers found **fewer false notes**. Experiment 4: reducing transportation reduced belief change.

Limits they own: the dependent variable is **persuasion / beliefs**, not L2 intake. The “false notes” finding is a caution for Davio: deep story absorption can **suppress** detection of anomalies. Schmidt-noticing needs conscious registration of a form. Transportation and Noticing can compete for attention. That is an implication, not a finding Green & Brock measured in German class.

### Motivation and memorability

Stories can raise willingness to keep reading (Elley; Krashen compelling; Nation’s meaning-focused input conditions). Transportation is a measured mechanism for *being lost in a text*. Neither programme measured “story vs list” for **B1–B2 German case morphology**. Vocabulary from extensive reading is a small-per-meeting, high-quantity effect (Nation, 2007; Waring & Takaki, 2003, via [01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md)) — plot helps you keep reading, which supplies meetings; the plot is not a memory palace for *dem*.

**Translation for Davio.** Interest/compellingness is already the two-week bar. A plotted Activity is legitimate as **meaning-focused input**. It is not a substitute for Noticing, and “because StoryLearning” is not a citation.

## 4. Cultural learning / intercultural communicative competence

### Pedagogy — Byram: ICC is five *savoirs*, not a culture lesson

Byram (1997), *Teaching and Assessing Intercultural Communicative Competence*, owns **intercultural communicative competence** as the goal of becoming an **intercultural speaker**, not a native-speaker clone. The model (as Byram restates it, including Byram, 2012, quoting 1997, p. 53) has:

- **savoirs** — knowledge of social groups and their products/practices in one’s own and the interlocutor’s country, and of interaction;
- **savoir être** — curiosity, openness, readiness to suspend disbelief about other cultures and belief about one’s own;
- **savoir comprendre** — skills of interpreting a document/event from another culture and relating it to one’s own;
- **savoir apprendre/faire** — skills of discovering new cultural knowledge and operating in real time;
- **savoir s’engager** — **critical cultural awareness**: “evaluate, critically and on the basis of explicit criteria, perspectives, practices and products in one’s own and other cultures and countries” (Byram, 1997, p. 53).

Byram (2012) puts *savoir s’engager* at the centre because it is the **educational** dimension. Skills, attitudes, and knowledge without critique can be trained commercially without that dimension. The 1997 book was not opened in full here; definitions above follow Byram’s later restatement and page-numbered quotations.

**Embedded vs “culture lesson.”** Byram’s object is competence in **encounters and documents**, not a Friday Landeskunde slide of “Germans are punctual.” A Gym text that is actually about a public debate, a regional practice, or a media stereotype can host interpreting/relating (savoir comprendre) *inside* reading. A disconnected quiz of festivals is the thing Kramsch was writing against.

### Pedagogy — CEFR Companion Volume: pluricultural repertoire

The CEFR already treated the learner as a **social agent** with an interrelated repertoire, not separate mental boxes for each language/culture (Council of Europe, 2001, §1.3, restated 2020). The 2020 Companion Volume adds scales. **Building on pluricultural repertoire** is about **skills in a communicative situation**, “rather than knowledge or attitudes” (Council of Europe, 2020, ch. 4). Key operationalised ideas include dealing with ambiguity, understanding that practices/norms differ, **discussing over-generalisations and stereotypes**, recognising similarities, and adjusting to sociolinguistic cues.

Progression they own (same chapter):

- **A levels:** recognise culturally based complications; simple everyday exchanges (greetings, thanks).
- **B1:** generally respond to commonly used cultural cues; explain features of own/other culture in simple terms; discuss how “strange” things may be “normal” for others.
- **B2:** describe and evaluate viewpoints and practices of own and other social groups; **awareness of implicit values on which judgments and prejudices are frequently based**; explain stereotypes and prejudices of own and other communities; interpret a document/event from another culture and relate it; generally interpret cultural cues; reflect on misunderstanding risks.
- **C:** explain backgrounds to beliefs/values; cope with sociolinguistic/pragmatic ambiguity.

The related scale **Facilitating pluricultural space** sits under mediation (creating conditions for openness), with Council of Europe webinar notes pointing to Byram’s critical cultural awareness and Kramsch’s third space (Council of Europe CEFR webinar background, 2021, citing Byram, 1997, 2008; Kramsch, 1993, 2002). FREPA/CARAP is named as a complementary, **non-levelled** inventory of savoir / savoir-être / savoir-faire.

CEFR **Sociolinguistic appropriateness** (2020) is a different scale: politeness, register, sociocultural cues. B1: common exponents in a **neutral register**; awareness of salient politeness conventions; looks out for significant differences in customs, usages, attitudes, values, beliefs. B2: modify expression for the situation; formal/informal register (not always successfully); avoid crass errors. Dialect and accent are listed as sociolinguistic matters; **unfamiliar accent** is a C1 reception hedge, not a B1 target.

### Pedagogy — Kramsch: third place, not national traits

Kramsch (1993), *Context and Culture in Language Teaching*, introduced **third place** as a pedagogic metaphor: a space where learners develop interculturality rather than consume “C2 facts.” Kramsch later restated that culture-as-object and nation-state polarity were the problem, and shifted toward **symbolic competence** (Kramsch, 2006; Kramsch, 2011, *Language Teaching*). The 1993 volume was not opened here; the owner of the later restatement is Kramsch (2011): intercultural work is discourse-based, historically grounded, aesthetically sensitive — not “Germans are like this.” MacDonald (2019) quotes Kramsch (1993, p. 216) that cultures also include age, gender, regional origin, ethnic background, and social class.

### Risks of stereotyping and essentialism

Owners, not vibes:

- CEFR CV: explicitly include **discussing over-generalisations and stereotypes**; B2 descriptors make prejudice/stereotype *objects of talk*, not teaching content to be memorised (Council of Europe, 2020).
- Byram: critical evaluation on **explicit criteria**, including one’s own practices (1997, p. 53).
- Kramsch: do not reduce culture to national traits; later, do not treat C1/C2 as bounded wholes (1993 via Kramsch, 2011).
- Auer (2013) on German varieties: **enregisterment** constructs “how people like us / like them are”; it is “not about real life” in the sense of actual isoglosses. Teaching “Austrians say X” as a personality fact is that ideology.

**UNVERIFIED** and therefore not to be shipped as Gym truth: how one “must” order coffee in Berlin vs Vienna vs Zürich; whether you “tut” or “Sie” in a given shop; which cake is “the” Austrian one. Those are the essentialism trap. If a piece *quotes* a character ordering, that is content. If the app asserts a sociolinguistic rule, it needs an owner (and still may be register/region-specific).

**Translation for Davio.** Culture belongs **inside** interesting texts (a character, a media row, a regional standard word) plus optional Noticing of *register/variety*, not as a parallel “culture tree.” B1–B2 CEFR already expects noticing difference and (at B2) talking about stereotypes. Do not build Landeskunde flashcards.

## 5. German regional variation (Language first)

### Language — Standard vs dialect; three (or more) *standards*

Two distinctions that product copy collapses:

1. **Dialect (non-standard regional speech) vs Standard German.** These are not “casual vs formal.” A Swiss learner’s Alemannic dialect and **Swiss Standard German** (used in writing, news, school) are different objects. IDS treats everyday spoken German as highly variable and describes a **Gebrauchsstandard** — a descriptive “usage standard” in formal-ish speech — not only the dictionary/grammar **Kodex** (IDS project *Gesprochenes Deutsch*; Elspaß & Dürscheid, 2017, on Gebrauchsstandard vs Kodex). The *Atlas zur Aussprache des deutschen Gebrauchsstandards* maps pronunciation of that usage standard, not “dialects as the real German.”

2. **National/areal varieties of Standard German.** German is treated as **pluricentric**: more than one national centre with codified standard varieties. Ammon (1995), *Die deutsche Sprache in Deutschland, Österreich und der Schweiz*, owns the national-varieties problem. The **Variantenwörterbuch des Deutschen** (Ammon et al., 2004; 2nd ed. Ammon, Bickel, & Lenz, 2016) documents **standard** lexical variants in the three “full centres” (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) plus “half centres” (Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, East Belgium, South Tyrol), expanded in 2016 to further German-speaking communities. It is **not** a dialect dictionary: common German words (*Baum*, *Frau*) are not full entries; only nationally/regionally marked standard lexicon is (Bickel & Schmidlin, on the first edition’s design).

**Pluricentric vs pluriareal.** Elspaß and Dürscheid (2017) report the live disagreement: national “centres” (Ammon) vs **areal** distributions that cross borders (a Bavarian–Austrian continuum, etc.). They note that even the VWB, while theoretically pluricentric, maps **15 areas**, not three states. Variantengrammatik (Dürscheid, Elspaß, Ziegler; corpus of ~600 million words from 68 regional newspapers; https://mediawiki.ids-mannheim.de/VarGra/) documents **grammatical** variation in **written Gebrauchsstandard** (e.g. *ist/hat gelegen*, *Wissenschafter/Wissenschaftler*) by area, not “wrong German.” Auer (2013) argues that some “Helvetisms/Austriacisms” are **enregistered** (stereotyped as national) even when the same feature exists in a German region.

**CEFR as reception target, not a dialect course.** B1–B2 listening/reading descriptors repeatedly require **“standard language or a familiar variety”** (Council of Europe, 2020). Unfamiliar accent is a C1 hedge. Sociolinguistic competence includes dialect and accent as *dimensions*, not as the Study-band syllabus. Teaching Bairisch as v1 content is out of band; **recognising that Austrian Standard *Semmel* / Swiss Standard *parkieren* are standard, not errors**, is a Language fact the Gym can respect when it appears.

### Pedagogy

DaF materials historically under-represent Austrian and Swiss **standard** variants (de Cillia, 2014, on curricula; Goethe-Institut teacher materials on Plurizentrik as a **receptive** aim). Hägi (cited in Goethe Plurizentrik slides): a pluricentric language has several national centres with different codified standards. Asymmetry they state: D-variants are more widely known in A/CH than vice versa, so D-standard can feel “neutral” — an ideology, not a linguistic ranking.

**UNVERIFIED:** any app copy that says “in Germany you must say X in shops.” Variation exists; the VWB/Variantengrammatik are the lookup, not a tourist blog.

**Translation for Davio.** Owned Gym German can be one consistent standard (likely D-standard for a Learner in Germany) **without** marking A/CH forms as mistakes if they appear in Immersion. Do not ship dialect as the Study band. Do not invent coffee-counter scripts. If a piece uses *Jänner* or *Velo*, a Noticing note can say “Austrian/Swiss **standard** variant” and point at VWB — Pedagogy overlay on a Language fact.

## 6. Place-based / authentic materials in CALL

### Pedagogy — authenticity is about *production*, not GPS

Gilmore (2007), *Language Teaching, 40*(2), 97–118, https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444807004144, reviews authenticity and lists **eight** different uses of the word. To keep it usable he adopts Morrow (1977, p. 13): an authentic text is “a stretch of real language, produced by a real speaker or writer for a real audience and designed to convey a real message of some sort.” Soap opera, teacher talk, and motherese all pass that test and have **different** surface features. Authenticity ≠ good; contrivance ≠ bad (Widdowson, Cook, cited in Gilmore). Hutchinson and Waters (1987, p. 159, quoted by Gilmore): ask **what role the text should play**, not “is it authentic?”

Gilmore’s classroom study (PhD, Japanese university): authentic audiovisual/web/print input plus noticing tasks beat two common textbooks on several communicative-competence measures; learners preferred authentic materials as more interesting and closer to perceived future needs. That is **discourse richness + motivation**, not “Tokyo geography vs fictional city.”

Meurers’s **ATICALL** (already in [14](14-linguistic-learning-content-application-domains.md)): NLP on authentic *native texts* to search/enhance activities. The “authentic” object is the **text**, not the pin on a map.

Davio already owns the **copyright** constraint: stored Activities are owner-written or rewritten; third-party news is link-out ([08](08-b1-b2-german-content-sources.md)). “Authentic Berlin newspaper” is often the wrong licence *and* the wrong coverage band (Hu & Nation, 2000).

### Place-based: real geography vs fiction

**Finding:** no primary study opened for this note shows that setting a lesson in **real** Berlin/Vienna (real streets, real cafés) beats a **fictional** but equally coherent setting for L2 acquisition or retention. Place-based education (Gruenewald and others) is a general-education literature about local curriculum; it was not followed here into an L2 RCT, and should not be borrowed as if it were.

What *is* owned: interest and relevance help people keep reading (Krashen, 2011; Elley & Mangubhai, 1983; Gilmore, 2007, motivation section). For a Learner **who lives in Germany**, a text about a recognisable public topic may be more compelling than a fake town — that is an **interest** hypothesis, not a geography-memory effect. A tourist map of Berlin for someone who already commutes there can also be boring. Unverified either way as a retention law.

**Translation for Davio.** Prefer **fitness to the Gym job** (Study-band coverage, Noticing target, interestingness, rights) over “authentic place.” A real toponym is fine as flavour. It is not a learning engine.

## 7. Game-design exploration / discovery / fog of war

### What MDA actually claimed

Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek (2004), “MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research” (AAAI workshop; GDC Game Design and Tuning Workshop 2001–2004). **Mechanics** = data, algorithms, actions afforded. **Dynamics** = run-time behaviour given player input. **Aesthetics** = desirable emotional responses. Games are artifacts whose content is **behaviour**, not the media stream.

Their non-exhaustive aesthetic taxonomy includes **Discovery: “Game as uncharted territory.”** *The Sims* and *Final Fantasy* are listed with Discovery among other aesthetics. Their babysitter-tag example: for young children, “Exploration and discovery are probably more important than challenge.” Fog of war in a military-sim pass is a **mechanic** chosen to support fantasy/challenge for a different audience — not a universal retention loop.

MDA does **not** claim that discovery retains players after novelty fades. The Monopoly worked example is about **runaway feedback** *killing* investment over time. Tuning is iterative playtest, not “keep adding fog.”

### Bartle (1996): explorers in MUDs

Bartle, “Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs.” Four types from two axes (acting vs interacting; players vs world): **Achievers, Explorers, Socialisers, Killers**. Explorers “dig around for information”; they want the game to expose internal machinations and to surprise them. The paper is about **balancing MUD populations**, not learning apps, and not single-player fog of war. Types are a folk taxonomy from MUD admins, not a factor analysis.

### Quantic Foundry / Yee (primary)

Yee (2006, Daedalus / “Motivations of Play in MMORPGs”) factor-analysed MMO motivations and **split Bartle’s Explorer**: geographical exploration and tinkering with mechanics **did not load together**. Motivations are scores, not exclusive types; Bartle’s axes wrongly assume that being more Achiever means being less Socialiser.

Quantic Foundry’s Gamer Motivation Profile (Yee, 2015a, 2015b; first-party blog): factor analysis on large survey samples. Early version: **no coherent Exploration factor**; map exploration loaded on **Fantasy**, mechanics exploration on **Mastery**. v2 added **Discovery** — “experimenting with the game world… constantly asking ‘what if?’” — clustered with **Design** under **Creativity**, not as “uncover the map.” Completion (“discover hidden things”) sits under **Achievement**. These are **self-reported importance ratings**, not behavioural retention curves, and not language-learning outcomes.

### Does exploration retain after novelty fades? Any learning-app evidence?

**Not in the owners above.** Discovery is an aesthetic some players want. Yee’s own first pass failed to find a stable “explore the map” factor. Hunicke et al. do not report longitudinal retention. **No language-app trial** of fog-of-war / map-unlock vs a normal Gym sitting was found for this note.

A reasonable **design** caution (not a citation): MDA says small mechanic changes cascade; novelty of tiles can be a Sensation/Discovery spike that dies when the map is complete — the same shape as Monopoly’s late-game deadness, but **UNVERIFIED** as a measured language-app effect.

**Translation for Davio.** Exploration can be a reason an adult opens a sitting (Discovery aesthetic). It is not evidence that a fog-of-war German map teaches case or retains at week three. Do not confuse Bartle “Explorer” with Lave “peripheral participant.”

## 8. Adaptive learning / personalization / session time

### What “the system knows what I need today” requires

Already split in [14](14-linguistic-learning-content-application-domains.md); restated with the same owners.

| Research object | Owner | What it needs | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **IRT** | Lord (1980); Rasch (1960) | Many persons × many items to put ability and difficulty on one scale | A CEFR tag on a lemma; a one-Learner hobby |
| **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing** | Corbett & Anderson (1995) | Fine-grained, relatively independent skills; many practice opportunities; guess/slip parameters | “Mastered the dative”; explanation-in-context sittings |
| **Mastery learning** | Bloom (1968), *Learning for Mastery*; DeKeyser SAT (1998/2007) | Time-to-criterion on a defined skill; practice until automatized (strong-interface story) | A boolean on a German ontology; Davio’s weak-interface bet ([01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md)) |

Krashen (1982, Input Hypothesis part 3) and Ellis (2005): exact `i+1` targeting is **impractical**; roughly-tuned input plus the internal syllabus do more than a structure-of-the-day. Pienemann teachability (via Lightbown, 2000) is stricter: instruction only helps at the next processable stage — and teachers still cannot cheaply know that stage.

**v1 verdict (same as 14):** do not build IRT/BKT. The Gap map filled by probes and self-marks is monitoring in Holec’s sense, not a psychometric engine.

### Contrast: interest-based personalization

Different object. Holec (1981): autonomy = take charge of objectives, contents, methods, monitoring. Ellis (2005, Principle 9) / Dörnyei via Ellis: quality of the teaching/content is the motivational intervention. Krashen (2011): compelling beats merely educational. Nation (2007): meaning-focused input only works if the Learner **wants** to understand the message.

Matching **today’s interest** (crime story vs grammar game) is a **content** choice. Matching **today’s gap** is a **learning** choice. Neither requires a city map. Combining them without data is the founder hypothesis already labelled in `CONTEXT.md`.

### Session design: microlearning vs longer sittings

Do not confuse three things:

1. **Spacing vs massing** (same total time, different gaps). Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer (2006): spacing beats massing for verbal recall; optimal gap grows with desired retention interval. Nakata (2015): for L2 vocab, **amount** of spacing is the large effect. Serrano (2022) review of L2 distribution-of-practice: repeating items inside one session should not be blocked/massed; **several sessions beat one session** for vocabulary; ideal inter-session lag is **not settled**. This is about **when** you return, not whether the sitting is 5 or 40 minutes.

2. **Time on task / quantity of input.** Nation (2007): extensive reading needs serious weekly volume (Nation & Wang, 1999, via Nation: on the order of a graded reader a week for repetition). Elley & Mangubhai’s gains came from **regular story reading over months**, not from snack-size drills. A five-minute sitting can be a legitimate *slice* if it is retrieval or a short text; it cannot substitute for Nation’s meaning-focused quantity.

3. **Consumer “microlearning.”** The 5-minute app lesson is a **product format**. It is not Cepeda, and it is not a demonstrated superior method for **B1–B2 connected text and form-in-context**. Studies that split 60 minutes into three 20-minute vocabulary classes (e.g. Namaziandost-type EFL vocab papers) are still **language-focused item teaching**, often with school-age learners. They do not speak to a phone Gym whose job is reading a piece and noticing *dem*.

Intensive vs extensive **programmes** (hours per week compressed vs spread) are another literature (Serrano’s review flags mixed lag results). Not the same as “microlearning.”

**Translation for Davio.** Design sittings that fit the phone bar (voluntary return) **and** still deliver a meaning-focused text plus Noticing — [08](08-b1-b2-german-content-sources.md) already used 5–15 min phone / 20–40 min desk as a **UX constraint**, not as a learning law. Prefer returning tomorrow (spacing + retrieval) over cramming ten clozes tonight. Do not let “the algorithm knows you only have four minutes” become a Focus-on-Forms snack that never reads.

## Implications for Davio

Labelled as recommendations, not requirements. This is not a UX spec.

- **Do not call a map, a café, or fog of war “situated learning.”** Lave & Wenger own participation in a practice. Immersion already is the world. The Gym is Focus on Form in texts ([01](01-sla-adult-self-directed.md)).
- **Do not rebuild Threshold situations for a B1–B2 Learner who lives here.** Café/train is van Ek’s T-level grid. Long-tasks need a needs analysis this hobby does not have. CLIL is school subjects. Nation-context is **new interesting texts**, plus capped language-focused work.
- **Stories are a legitimate interest engine**, owned as compelling input / book floods / transportation. They are not a measured grammar method for this band, and transportation can **fight** Noticing (Green & Brock, false notes). StoryLearning® is not a source.
- **Culture = ICC / pluricultural skills in texts**, not Landeskunde cards. Stereotype critique is a B2 CEFR skill. **UNVERIFIED** coffee-counter rules stay out.
- **Language: dialects ≠ Austrian/Swiss Standard.** Look up variants in VWB / Variantengrammatik / IDS. CEFR Study-band reception is standard language (or a variety the Learner already knows).
- **Authentic place is not a retention finding.** Gilmore authenticity is how the text was produced. Rights and coverage still rule ([08](08-b1-b2-german-content-sources.md)).
- **Exploration is an MDA aesthetic some players like**, not evidence it retains after novelty, and not a language-learning RCT.
- **Adaptive “needs today” is IRT/BKT/mastery on items** — out of v1 identifiability ([14](14-linguistic-learning-content-application-domains.md)). Interest-matching is the other personalization object. Spacing across days beats massing; **short vs long sitting is not that finding**.

**Founder approval** is needed only if the destination changes toward a map-led, culture-track, or adaptive-engine product. RESUME.md currently forbids rediscovering that destination. This note’s research recommendation is: **stay with owned texts, Noticing, Gap map**; treat story, place, and variety as properties of those texts.
